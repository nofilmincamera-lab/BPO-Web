Decisions before build

Five open calls. Recommendations are marked; D1 is a deliberate departure from a stated storyboard rule.

D1

Does the camera scale to the active stage through the middle, or hold wide for all 115 seconds?

Scale. The cognitive-load rule was written for 60 seconds; a fixed wide shot for 115 reads as static, and the 2.76 s pull-back at 102.72 s is wasted without it. Composition and stage order never change, which is what that rule actually protects.

D2

The outcome is an approval, not a fraud finding. Is false-positive cost the argument we want leading?

Yes — and say so in the deck. It is the stronger commercial argument for an evaluator who already owns the fraud-loss case. But note the film does not demonstrate a confirmed-suspicion path. If evaluators need that, it is a second asset, not an extension of this one.

D3

QA gets 2.00 s of silence and no narration. Accept, or record a VO pickup?

Accept. Three 300 ms checks fit with room. A pickup would break the read's rhythm and the ring composition. If QA must carry more weight for a specific evaluator, give it a dedicated PDF frame rather than more screen time.

D4

“Clears straight through” is stronger than the storyboard's permitted learning register.

Keep the audio; carry the qualification on screen. The governance label beside the return path is mandatory and must hold legible for the full 5.8 s of Shot 17. Do not re-record.

D5

Is the customer ribbon in scope?

Yes. One line of persistent furniture, and the only way “the customer never felt the review” lands as evidence rather than assertion. It has to be unbroken for ninety seconds before it can pay off in three.

Build order

Not chronological. Build the shots that carry the argument first, so a failure is found while it is still cheap.

  1. Shot 12 — the inversion. The film's central idea. If the chip reorder doesn't read, nothing downstream matters.
  2. Shot 14 — the boundary. The governance proof, and the shot an RFP evaluator will scrub back to.
  3. Shots 2 + 10. Record card and its strike-throughs — one component, two states. Build together.
  4. Shot 17. The learning loop, with the governance label locked in from the first pass.
  5. Shot 3 + persistent architecture. The three-chip component and all stationary furniture.
  6. Shots 1 + 19. The ring — one composition in two states, never two frames.
  7. Everything else.

Acceptance checks

Carried from the storyboard's production checklist. Mint boxes are the four this cut adds.

  • The proposition is legible within five seconds.
  • Stage order and composition never change; only scale and the case move.
  • Foundever is visibly absent from the clean automated path.
  • L1 and L2 have distinct purposes — L1 structures, L2 resolves sequence.
  • The authority boundary is a hard line, and the token does not cross it.
  • QA resolves before closure, not after.
  • The learning loop returns insight to Klarna-controlled automation, label legible throughout.
  • The second case completes without starting a new story.
  • Every beat has a clean, printable settled frame.
  • The three signals invert rather than being replaced — same chips, reordered and recoloured.
  • The decline column never resolves into a state — ghosted and dashed for its full life.
  • The customer ribbon is unbroken from 0:05 to 118:00, and brightens exactly once.
  • Shots 1 and 19 are the same composition in two states, not two different frames.

Final comprehension test. Show frame 8 for five seconds, then hide it. The viewer should be able to say: “Klarna's automation flags what it should. Foundever resolves the judgment. Klarna keeps the decisions that are Klarna's. QA gates the close. And the next case is better informed.”
